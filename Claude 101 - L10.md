Source: the lesson page. The "In simple words" lines and analogies marked "my own" are mine. The page does not say which plans include Research (it only says web search must be on), so I'm not stating plan limits; I'll check my own account.

**Learning goals**
Explain what Research does: systematic, multi-source investigation.
Know when to use Research for gathering lots of information.
Understand how Research uses Thinking to plan before gathering information.
Write effective Research prompts for complex investigations.

1. **What is Research**?

**In simple words**: a mode where Claude acts like a detective-researcher instead of a quick answerer. It plans, runs many searches, follows leads, and writes a report with sources.

Analogy (my own): a normal web search is like asking a librarian one question. Research is like sending a research assistant to the library for the afternoon, and getting a finished report back.

**From the lesson**:

It's an advanced feature that turns Claude from a conversational assistant into a systematic investigator.
It explores a question from multiple angles, combining information from the web and connected integrations.
It's like a research assistant who gathers information, cross-checks sources, and compiles a report while I work on other things.
It suits questions needing more than a quick answer: comparing perspectives and turning findings into actionable insights.

2. **Key takeaways** (from the lesson)
Research changes how Claude finds and analyzes information. Instead of one search, Claude works agentically (on its own, step by step), running several searches that build on each other and deciding what to look into next.
It takes longer than a normal search: a few minutes or more. Claude can send out many searches at once, sometimes across hundreds of sources, then combine the results. (Lesson 2 said about 5 to 45 minutes.)
It works with Thinking, so Claude can plan first by splitting a complex request into manageable pieces.
Citations make checking easy. Answers come with sources I can verify.

3. **How Research works** (4 steps)
Step	                What happens
1. **Plan**	             Claude breaks down my request, works out what it needs, and plans how to look into each angle
2. **Search**	           Claude runs many searches that build on each other, following promising leads and filling gaps
3. **Synthesize**	       It combines the findings from the web and connected tools (Gmail, Google Calendar, Google Drive) into an organized report
4. **Cite**	             Every claim links back to its source. The page says: "Every claim in Research reports links back to its source."

4. **When to use what**
Use Research for
Comprehensive reports that combine many sources
In-depth analysis across the web and connected tools (e.g. Google Workspace)
Investigations that would otherwise take hours of manual work
Comparisons (competitors, vendor options)
Reports with checkable citations

Typical tasks: market analysis and competitive research; planning complex projects (team offsites, product launches); combining email, calendar, and documents; technical documentation from multiple sources; briefings needing current, verified information.

Use something else when...
If I need...	Use
A quick, specific fact (stock price, company address), one or two sources, or speed over completeness	Web search
Deep reasoning but no outside information (math, code debugging, logic)	Thinking
Answers from internal company knowledge (documents, Slack threads, emails, meeting notes), or learning company policies and past decisions	Enterprise search (Lesson 9)

Memory trick (my own): Research = wide and cited. Web search = quick fact. Thinking = deep reasoning. Enterprise search = inside the company.

5. **Using Research** (4 steps)
Click the + button at the bottom left of the chat.
Select Research from the menu. It shows highlighted when active.
Type the prompt and submit.
Claude works in the background and shows progress indicators while it searches and analyzes.

Important: Web search must be turned on for Research to work. It can be turned on from the same + menu.

6. **Tips for effective Research prompts**
Tip                                   	Example from the lesson
**Be specific about goals**	                       Instead of "Tell me about the EV market," ask for key players, technology trends, and supply chain challenges that affect investment decisions
**Specify sections or structure**	                 Claude organizes findings around the structure I give. Example: a team offsite venue comparison covering location and accessibility, meeting space and amenities, catering, and pricing
**Include constraints**	                           Budget ranges, timelines, and geographic requirements focus the research
**Ask Claude to help refine the prompt**	         If I'm unsure how to frame the question, ask Claude to help write the Research prompt first, before turning Research on

(This is the Description skill from AI Fluency: Product, Process, Performance.)

My own example (weak vs. strong)
Weak: "Tell me about AI security jobs."
Strong: "I'm a beginner moving from inside sales into AI security. Research entry-level roles, the skills each needs, and free ways to learn them (goal). Organize it into Roles, Skills, Free resources, and a 3-month starter plan (structure). Focus on India-based or remote opportunities and free or low-cost options (constraints). Cite every source (rules)."

7. **Working with connected integrations**

With Google Workspace or other integrations connected, Claude can pull context from my emails, calendar, and documents alongside web research.

Example prompts from the lesson:

"Summarize what has been discussed about Project X across email and Slack, then research industry best practices for similar initiatives."
"Review my calendar commitments for next week and research each company I'm meeting with."
"Find internal documents about the pricing strategy and compare them to how competitors position themselves."

Steering phrases: "Pull relevant context from my Google Drive" or "Include insights from my recent emails on this topic."

Research is for multi-source, cited, comprehensive investigations that take minutes instead of hours. Use it when a question needs combining many sources. Use web search, Thinking, or enterprise search for simpler or different needs.

**Quick revision**
Research = Claude plans, runs many connected searches, combines results, and cites every claim.
Steps: Plan, Search, Synthesize, Cite.
Takes minutes or more; sometimes hundreds of sources; works in the background.
Turn on: + (bottom left) > Research. Web search must be on.
Better prompts: specific goal, structure, constraints, or ask Claude to help write the prompt.
Not Research: quick fact = web search; reasoning only = Thinking; company-internal = enterprise search.
Connected tools (Gmail, Calendar, Drive) can add personal context.
Link to my goals
Everyday use: great for big decisions (choosing a course, comparing options) where I'd normally spend hours.
AI Security (later):
Citations help me check the answers, but a citation is a claim, not proof. I should open the sources (Discernment).
Research reads many web pages, and any page could contain hidden instructions (prompt injection). More sources means more places to be tricked.
When connected tools are included, Research can mix private emails and documents with public web content. That raises data privacy questions (Diligence). The page gives no specific security guidance for Research, and only notes that it can draw from connected accounts, so I should think carefully about what I connect.
Hundreds of sources can include low-quality or manipulated ones, so source quality matters.
